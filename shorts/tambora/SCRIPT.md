## Facts (sources)
- **10 April 1815 — Tambora (Sumbawa, modern Indonesia).** Largest eruption in recorded history. VEI 7; ~150 km³ of
  rock and ash ejected (bulk); eruption column ~43 km into the stratosphere; ~1,400 m of summit removed; ~10–11k
  direct deaths; at least ~71k total (mostly famine/disease afterwards). [Smithsonian/volcanodb; Oppenheimer 2003; Wikipedia]
- **"Dry fog" over the US during summer 1815** — a stratospheric sulfate aerosol veil; sun reddened and dimmed;
  **sunspots visible to the naked eye**. [Historical accounts; Encyclopaedia Britannica]
- **1816 = "The Year Without a Summer."** Global temperatures fell ~0.4–0.7 °C. Frosts hit New England in June 1816
  (Albany, NY, 6 June; ~46 cm snow cover reported in Cabot, VT, 8 June). Harvests failed across the Northern Hemisphere;
  Europe suffered the worst famine of the 19th century; food riots (Germany, Switzerland). Nickname in New England:
  **"eighteen hundred and froze to death."** US grain prices ~quadrupled; **oat prices rose ~8x**. [NOAA; almanac.com; Wikipedia]
- **Frankenstein.** June 1816, Villa Diodati, Lake Geneva: Byron's ghost-story dare; ~18-year-old Mary Godwin
  (later Mary Shelley), rained in for days, wrote the first draft of *Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*
  (published 1818). Polidori → *The Vampyre* (→ Dracula). [British Library; Wikipedia; National Geographic]
- **The horse shortage.** Harvest failures drove fodder prices to ruin; horses were starved, slaughtered or eaten;
  personal transport collapsed for non-elites. [scihub101.com; deutsche.de/en; Wikipedia (draisienne)]
- **Karl Friedrich Christian Ludwig Freiherr Drais von Sauerbronn (1785–1851)**, baron and forest inspector
  (Forstmeister) in the Grand Duchy of Baden, built the **Laufmaschine** ("running machine") — wooden, ~22 kg,
  steerable front wheel, **no pedals**. **12 June 1817:** first public ride, Mannheim → Schwetzingen, ~13–14 km in
  under an hour (~15 km/h avg), faster than walking. Patents: Baden (12 Jan 1818), France (17 Feb 1818, "vélocipède").
  [deutschland.de; camcycle.org.uk; wikipedia]
- **The bans.** Mannheim banned pavement riding in Dec 1817 (citywide except the castle garden); Milan and Paris 1818;
  London, New York and Calcutta 1819. Riders kept using footways and colliding with pedestrians. [HMDB; Wikipedia]
- **What followed.** Pedal-crank velocipedes (Michaux et al.) in the 1860s; chain drive 1885 (Starley "safety");
  bicycle became the most-ridden vehicle on Earth. [scihub101.com timeline]

## Beat sheet (draft v2 — ~265 words)

A volcano erased summer — and ended up inventing the bicycle.

April 1815. Mount Tambora, on the island of Sumbawa, goes off — the biggest eruption in recorded history.
A hundred and fifty cubic kilometres of rock and ash, thrown forty kilometres into the sky.

It killed more than seventy thousand people, mostly of hunger — and it changed the weather of the whole planet.

A year later came 1816 — the year without a summer. June brought snow to New England.
Harvests failed from Ireland to China. They called it eighteen hundred and froze to death.
Oat prices rose eight times.

And on Lake Geneva, rained in at the Villa Diodati, an eighteen-year-old named Mary Shelley got so bored she wrote Frankenstein.

Meanwhile, in Baden, a forester and baron named Karl Drais had the same problem.
The oats were gone — and with them, the horses.

So he built a better horse. Two wheels, a beam, a saddle.
No pedals — a running machine. The Laufmaschine. You push off the ground with your feet.

On June the twelfth, 1817, he rode from Mannheim to Schwetzingen — fourteen kilometres in under an hour, on wood, with zero horses.

Luckily, it worked.
Unluckily, the riders didn't.
Mannheim banned them from the pavements. Milan, London and New York followed.

Because a volcano is just a chimney that burps a parasol into space.
Dim the sun a little, and the wheat gives up, the oats give up, the horse gives up — and the road is yours.

The bans didn't last. Pedals came in the 1860s, then chains, then gears — until a volcano's bad mood became the most-ridden vehicle on Earth.

So next time someone flies past on a bicycle — remember: that's Mount Tambora's fault.
One eruption. One lost summer. One man, no horse.
